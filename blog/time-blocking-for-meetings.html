Time Blocking When Your Day Is Full of Meetings

Standard time-blocking advice assumes empty mornings to arrange — useless for the calendar that is already 60% meetings. Blocking a meeting-heavy week is a different discipline: you block what remains, batch what you control, and treat gap-classes differently by size. Here is that version.

6 min read · Updated Jul 6, 2026 · Written by Sergey Ilyin

Invert the method: meetings are the terrain

Classic time blocking designs the ideal week and defends it. On a meeting-heavy calendar, the meetings are the terrain — you don’t design around them, you map them and block what the map leaves. The weekly sequence: first stabilize the meeting layer (below), then place focus blocks in the largest surviving spaces, then assign the small gaps by class. Attempting it in any other order produces the classic failure — beautiful focus blocks that meetings steamroll by Tuesday.

Step 1 · Stabilize the meeting layer

Two moves shape the terrain itself. Batch what you control: drag your own recurring meetings toward two designated heavy zones (say Tue/Thu 13:00–16:00) — every meeting moved out of a morning creates contiguous space that scattered 30-minute gaps never add up to. Shrink systematically: speedy-meetings defaults plus one pass converting your 60s to 45s. On a 15-meeting week, the shrink pass alone manufactures ~2.5 hours of new terrain.

Step 2 · Claim the large spaces (90+ minutes)

Whatever contiguous spaces survive — typically 2–4 per week after batching — get the production blocks: named outputs, +25% sizing, marked Busy. These are scarce assets on a meeting-heavy calendar, so two protections apply that lighter calendars don’t need: never schedule shallow work into a 90+ minute space (it is the only place deep work can live), and defend them at meeting rank — a reschedule-with-alternative for anything that lands on them, no exceptions in the first month.

Step 3 · Assign the gap classes

Meeting-heavy days generate gaps in three sizes, each with a correct use:

  • 10–15 minutes (between calls): meeting metabolism only — action-item capture from the last call, one-line prep for the next. Attempting real tasks here produces half-started work and late joins.
  • 25–45 minutes: the shallow-batch zone — email passes, reviews, small tickets, scheduling admin. Keep a standing "gap list" (three pre-chosen small tasks in your task manager) so these slots never spend themselves deciding.
  • The cancelled meeting (surprise 30–60): the trap slot. The reflex is email; the win is either extending an adjacent focus block or taking the recovery the day owes you. Decide the policy once — "cancellations attach to the nearest block or become breaks" — so the moment doesn’t re-litigate it.

The week this produces

Realistic shape for a 60%-meeting calendar: two mornings with 90–120 minute production blocks, meeting-dense Tuesday/Thursday afternoons, gap-list slots absorbing the shrapnel, and 10-minute seams doing meeting metabolism. Not the influencer’s color-coded utopia — but it ships two real deliverables a week from a calendar that previously shipped exhaustion.

Gap-class blocking runs on knowing the gap’s exact size the moment it opens — "23 minutes until the next call" routes you to the gap list, not the inbox. Key takeaways

  • Map the meeting terrain first; batch and shrink before placing any block.
  • 90+ minute spaces are scarce assets: deep work only, defended at meeting rank.
  • Gaps by class: seams = meeting metabolism, 25–45 = gap list, cancellations = pre-decided.
  • Two shipped deliverables a week is the honest win condition at 60% meeting load.
