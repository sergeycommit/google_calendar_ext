How to Use the Pomodoro Technique With Google Calendar

Google Calendar has no Pomodoro timer, and trying to make it one — 16 recurring 25-minute events a day — creates calendar sludge nobody maintains. The workable split: the calendar owns the sessions, a timer owns the intervals. Here is the setup, the interval math for different work types, and when to abandon 25/5 entirely.

6 min read · Updated Jul 21, 2026 · Written by Sergey Ilyin

The division of labor

Pomodoro has two layers that people conflate. The session — "90 minutes on the report this morning" — is a scheduling decision, and that belongs on the calendar where it blocks bookings and survives glances. The intervals — 25 on, 5 off, repeat — are execution rhythm, and they belong to a timer (phone, kitchen timer, tomato-shaped or otherwise). A calendar full of micro-events does the timer’s job badly and ruins the calendar’s legibility doing it.

Setup that takes five minutes

  1. Block sessions, not pomodoros. Create 60–120 minute focus blocks titled by outcome ("Draft §2 of proposal"), set to Busy. A 90-minute block holds three clean pomodoros (25+5 ×3); two hours holds four.
  2. Guard the block’s exit, not its interior. No notification at the block’s start (you planned it), one notification 10 minutes before whatever follows — the alert’s job is stopping pomodoro #4 from crashing into your 11:00.
  3. Run intervals on a real timer. Any countdown works. The only requirement: it is not a browser tab (tabs get buried; buried timers end sessions silently).
  4. Log honestly, lightly. After the block, edit the event description with one line: "3 poms, finished outline". Thirty seconds, and week view becomes a truthful record of focus capacity — the input your future planning actually needs.

Interval math by work type

25/5 is a default, not a law. The pattern that matters: interval length should match the cost of re-entering the task.

  • Shallow, resumable work (email passes, admin, review queues): classic 25/5. Re-entry is free, so frequent breaks cost nothing and the box-pressure helps.
  • Deep, state-heavy work (writing, debugging, design): 50/10 — the "double pomodoro". Loading the problem into your head takes 10–15 minutes; 25-minute slices spend half their length on re-entry.
  • Dread work (the thing avoided for a week): 15/5 to start. The interval’s only job is making the first contact survivable; upgrade to 25s once you’re moving.

Where calendar-Pomodoro fails, and the honest fixes

Meetings shred the grid. A day with five scattered calls has no room for clean cycles — run single pomodoros in the 30-minute gaps (one 25/5 fits exactly) rather than mourning the lost rhythm. Interruptions void the pomodoro. Orthodox rule, worth keeping: a broken interval restarts rather than resumes, which is precisely what makes colleagues’ "quick questions" visible as costs. The technique fights flow. When the 25 ends mid-breakthrough, the doctrine says stop; experience says the doctrine is wrong for creative peaks. Finish the thought, skip the break, and treat it as evidence you have outgrown boxes for this task — that is what focus blocks without intervals are for.

Between pomodoros, the recurring question is "do I have room for another cycle before the next commitment?" Key takeaways

  • Calendar owns sessions (60–120 min blocks); a timer owns the 25/5 intervals.
  • Match interval to re-entry cost: 25/5 shallow, 50/10 deep, 15/5 for dread work.
  • Alert the block’s exit, not its start; log cycles in the event description.
