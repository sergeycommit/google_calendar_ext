Why Buffer Time Between Meetings Is Not Optional

The 30-minute meeting is a lie: with mental wind-down and wind-up, it occupies 40-plus minutes of a human — and back-to-back scheduling bills that overdraft to your last meeting of the day. Buffers are how the schedule pays its own physiology tax. Here is the sizing, the mechanics, and the defense.

5 min read · Updated Sep 18, 2026 · Written by Sergey Ilyin

The physiology, briefly

Microsoft’s Human Factors Lab put numbers to what everyone feels (EEG, 14 participants, 2021): four consecutive 30-minute video meetings produced steadily climbing stress-associated brain activity, while the same meetings with 10-minute breaks kept the curve flat. Sophie Leroy’s attention-residue research (2009) adds the second half: task-switching leaves residue that lowers performance on the next task. A buffer is not slack; it is where the previous meeting finishes happening in your head.

Sizing: not all gaps are equal

  • 5 minutes — biological minimum between internal calls: water, stand, one breath. Speedy meetings (:25/:50 endings) manufactures these automatically.
  • 10–15 minutes — the working standard after any meeting that produced action items: write the three lines now or lose 40% of them by evening (the notes-decay effect every retro rediscovers).
  • 25–30 minutes — after external calls, negotiations, interviews, anything adversarial or performative: the wind-down is real, and the next meeting deserves a person, not an adrenaline echo.
  • Zero — legitimately fine between two low-stakes internal syncs on the same project. Buffers are a budget; spend them where switches are expensive.

Mechanics: make the calendar do it

Three layers, cheapest first. Layer 1 — defaults: Speedy meetings on, default duration 25; every meeting you create now ships with a built-in seam. Layer 2 — standing buffers: for your known heavy clusters (the Tuesday gauntlet), recurring 15-minute blocks titled "Buffer — notes & reset", marked Busy. Titled honestly, because mystery blocks get challenged and "Busy" gets booked. Layer 3 — the accept-time habit: when accepting anything back-to-back-making, add your own 10-minute block after it in the same gesture. Buffers created at accept-time cost nothing; buffers needed at 15:00 cost a negotiation.

Defending buffers without apologizing

Someone will book over "Buffer — notes & reset" and feel reasonable. The response that holds: treat it as a real conflict — "I have a commitment until 14:15, can we start then?" — because it is a real commitment: to the previous meeting’s follow-ups and the next one’s quality. Teams that watch a senior person defend buffers adopt them within a quarter; teams where buffers fold on first contact learn they were decorative.

The buffer dividend

Count what changes in a buffered week: action items actually captured (the 10-minute versions), meetings entered with the agenda re-read instead of the previous call still echoing, and — the quiet one — the 16:00 meeting getting the same person the 9:00 got. The cost is 30–60 minutes of grid space a day; the return is that your meeting hours start producing meeting outcomes.

Buffers work best when you can see them coming: knowing there’s a 10-minute seam after this call changes how you spend the call’s last five minutes. Frequently asked questions

Marked Busy, they’re protected from find-a-time; against manual over-booking, defense is a two-line reply (see above) applied consistently. If they still fold weekly, hide the title — a plain "Busy" buffer trades legibility for survival, an acceptable price in aggressive cultures.

Speedy endings cover the 5-minute biological tier. You still want the 10–15 after action-item meetings (writing follow-ups needs more than five) and the 25–30 after high-stakes externals. Think of speedy meetings as the floor, standing buffers as the insulation on known hot spots.

In order of payoff: write the action items (three lines, owners, dates), then physical reset (stand, water, eyes off screen), then — only if both are done — a glance at what’s next. What should not happen: email. Email is a context switch wearing a productivity costume.

The transition into a focus block benefits from a small ramp (5 minutes to close threads and silence pings), and the exit needs protection more than the entrance — a 10-minute notification before whatever follows the block prevents the overrun that turns deep work into an apology.

Key takeaways

  • A buffer is where the last meeting finishes happening — physiology, not slack.
  • Size by switch cost: 5 internal, 10–15 after action items, 25–30 after externals.
  • Create buffers at accept-time; they’re free then and expensive at 15:00.
  • Defend them as real commitments — one consistent senior defender re-norms a team.
