Why Knowing Time-to-Next-Event Changes How You Work

"When’s my next meeting?" is not really a time question — it is a permission question: can I start something deep, or only something shallow? Making time-to-next-event permanently visible changes how the space between meetings gets spent. Here is the mechanism, the decision rules it enables, and how to get the number ambiently.

5 min read · Updated Aug 26, 2026 · Written by Sergey Ilyin

The gap is a budget you can’t spend blind

Between commitments, every choice of what to do next is implicitly sized: starting a deep task with 12 minutes left is a write-off (you’ll pay the entry cost and be interrupted before producing), while spending a 70-minute gap on inbox grazing is the opposite waste. People without the number default to the safe-feeling choice — shallow work — because uncertainty about the gap makes deep starts feel risky. That is the quiet mechanism by which meeting-heavy calendars produce shallow days even in their gaps.

The decision rules a visible number enables

With "43 min until standup" ambient, gap-spending becomes rule-based instead of vibe-based:

  • 60+ minutes: a real deep-work session — enter properly, entry cost amortizes.
  • 25–60: one bounded unit: a single pomodoro on the current project, one review, one document pass. Not a "quick start" on something big.
  • 10–25: the gap list — pre-chosen shallow items that need no warm-up.
  • Under 10: meeting metabolism — capture last call’s actions, glance at the next agenda, water. Starting anything else is how you join late with half a thought open.

The rules are trivial; the number is the hard part. Nobody re-derives it 30 times a day by opening the calendar — which is why, without ambient visibility, everyone falls back to the shallow default.

The anxiety dividend

There is a second, quieter payoff. A large share of calendar checks are not information-seeking but reassurance-seeking — "I’m not missing anything, right?" — and each one costs a context switch. A standing countdown answers the reassurance question preemptively: the background process that used to interrupt with "check the calendar" goes quiet, because the answer is already in the corner of the screen. Users consistently describe this as the change they didn’t expect: not faster checks, but fewer.

Getting the number ambiently

Ranked by how well they serve the browser-based workday: a toolbar extension with countdown chips — Calendar Extension for Google Calendar™ shows "in 23 min" on each upcoming event, one click from any tab, which makes the gap rules executable mid-work; a menu-bar next-event app (the native-desktop equivalent); the phone widget (for away-from-desk moments); and — the weakest option — the pinned calendar tab, which has the information but demands a tab-switch to consult it, re-introducing the exact cost the number was meant to remove.

One habit to pair with it

The number changes behavior only when consulted before choosing the next action — so attach it there: end of any task or meeting, the sequence is glance → rule → go. Two weeks of that pairing and it runs itself; the visible countdown plus the four rules quietly re-allocates two or three formerly-shallow gaps a week into real sessions, which is most of what "getting more focus time" was ever going to mean on a meeting-heavy calendar.

Key takeaways

  • "When’s my next meeting" is really "what am I allowed to start" — a permission question.
  • Four gap rules (60+/25–60/10–25/<10) turn spacing into allocation.
  • The unexpected dividend is fewer checks: countdowns answer reassurance preemptively.
  • Pair the number with one habit: glance → rule → go, at every task boundary.
