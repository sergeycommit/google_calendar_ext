How to Build a Color-Coding System in Google Calendar

Color-coding fails when it decorates instead of answers. A good palette lets you answer one question per color with a squint — no reading, no clicking. Here is a six-color system built around the questions a week actually raises, plus the mechanics Google Calendar hides.

6 min read · Updated Jul 6, 2026 · Written by Sergey Ilyin

The squint test

Before assigning a single color, decide what question it answers. Zoom out to week view and squint: if the blur of colors can answer "how much of this week is spoken for by other people?", the palette is working. If it just looks like confetti, you have labels, not information.

A six-color system that answers real questions

ColorMeansQuestion it answers at a squint
TomatoExternal commitments — clients, interviews, anything with outside attendeesHow much of my week is promised to people who can't reschedule easily?
BlueberryInternal meetingsWhat is my meeting load, and which days stack?
BasilDeep work blocksDid focus time survive the week, or get eaten?
GrapePersonal — health, family, commuteIs life getting squeezed out by work weeks?
GraphiteAdmin, logistics, shallow choresHow much low-value time am I carrying?
BananaTentative — holds, unconfirmed plansWhat might still move if something urgent lands?

Six is a deliberate ceiling. Google Calendar offers more event colors, but every color past six halves the palette's readability — an eleven-color calendar and an uncolored one carry the same information: none.

Calendar colors vs. event colors

Google Calendar has two coloring layers, and mixing them up is the most common setup mistake:

  • Calendar color — set from the three-dot menu next to each calendar in the left sidebar. Everything on that calendar inherits it. Use it for source identity: your work calendar, a shared team calendar, a partner's overlay.
  • Event color — right-click any event (or open it and use the color dropdown). It overrides the calendar color for that one event. Use it for semantics — the six meanings above.

The system that scales: leave the calendar color as a neutral base, and apply event colors only when an event deviates from that calendar's default meaning. Coloring a recurring event once colors the whole series — right-click it, pick the color, and choose All events when prompted.

The two-minute Friday audit

The palette earns its keep in review. Friday afternoon, open week view and ask three questions in order: Is Basil visibly present on at least three days? Is any single day more than half Blueberry? Is Graphite creeping past roughly a fifth of the week? Each "wrong" answer maps to one concrete fix for next week — protect a block, break up a stacked day, batch the admin into one box.

What colors cannot do

Two limits worth knowing before you rely on the system. First, your colors are private — attendees see their own palette, so color carries no meaning in invites. Second, native Google Calendar cannot auto-color by rule (for example, "everything with 5+ guests turns Blueberry"). The workable native approximation is routing event types into separate calendars, which is exactly what calendar-level colors are for.

The palette also pays off at a glance mid-day: in a toolbar popup like Calendar Extension for Google Calendar™, the color strip on each upcoming event tells you whether what's next is a Tomato (prepare) or a Graphite (relax) before you read a single word.

Key takeaways
